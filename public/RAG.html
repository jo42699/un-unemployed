Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) Questions




Question 1

What is Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), and why is it important?

Answer: AI Engineering Explained: LLM, RAG, MCP, Agent, Fine-Tuning, Quantization. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is an architecture where a Transformer-based LLM is paired with a retrieval component that fetches relevant documents from an external knowledge store before generation. The user query is tokenized, embedded, and used to perform vector or hybrid search, and the retrieved context is injected into the model’s prompt so attention can focus on grounded information rather than relying purely on parametric memory. This reduces hallucinations, improves faithfulness, and allows smaller models to reason over large corpora without retraining, since the training weights handle language understanding and reasoning while retrieval supplies up-to-date facts. RAG is important because it decouples knowledge from the model’s training cycle, enabling continuous updates, domain adaptation, and more controllable sampling behavior with explicit citations.

Question 2

Explain the architecture of a basic RAG system.

Answer: AI Engineering Explained: LLM, RAG, MCP, Agent, Fine-Tuning, Quantization. A basic RAG architecture consists of a retriever, a knowledge store, and a generator (LLM) orchestrated by a controller. The retriever uses tokenization and an embedding model to convert queries and documents into vectors, then applies similarity search (e.g., cosine or dot-product) to select top-k chunks. These chunks, along with the original query, are concatenated into a prompt that the Transformer model processes with multi-head attention, allowing it to attend over both the question tokens and the retrieved context. During training or fine-tuning, the system may optimize retrieval quality and generation jointly, while at inference time sampling strategies like temperature, top-k, or nucleus sampling control how the LLM reasons over and verbalizes the retrieved information. The architecture can be extended with re-ranking, query transformation, and agentic planning components for more complex reasoning chains.

Question 3

What are the key components of a RAG pipeline?

Answer: AI Engineering Explained: LLM, RAG, MCP, Agent, Fine-Tuning, Quantization. A RAG pipeline typically includes ingestion, chunking, embedding, indexing, retrieval, context assembly, and generation. Ingestion parses raw documents (PDFs, HTML, tables) into clean text; chunking splits this text into token-length-aware segments that fit within the LLM’s context window. Embedding models transform each chunk into dense vectors that capture semantic meaning, which are stored in a vector database alongside metadata for filtering. At query time, the pipeline tokenizes the user input, embeds it, retrieves candidate chunks, optionally re-ranks them, and assembles a context prompt that the Transformer model consumes with attention. The generator then produces an answer using its learned reasoning capabilities, and post-processing may add citations, perform consistency checks, or apply sampling constraints to reduce hallucinations and improve reliability.

Question 4

What are chunking strategies, and how do you choose the right chunk size?

Chunking strategies define how documents are split into smaller units that can be embedded and retrieved efficiently while fitting within the LLM’s context window. Choosing chunk size requires balancing semantic coherence with token limits: too small and attention must span many fragments, increasing retrieval noise; too large and important details may be truncated or exceed the model’s maximum tokens. Practitioners often use token-based chunking (e.g., 256–1024 tokens) guided by the tokenizer of the target Transformer model, ensuring that each chunk aligns with natural boundaries like paragraphs or sections. The right size depends on the domain, typical question length, and reasoning depth required—complex multi-hop reasoning may benefit from slightly larger chunks plus parent-child relationships, while high-latency environments may prefer smaller chunks to speed up embedding and search. Empirical evaluation with retrieval metrics and downstream QA performance is essential to tune chunk size.

Question 5

Compare fixed-size chunking, semantic chunking, and recursive chunking.

Fixed-size chunking splits text into uniform token or character windows, making indexing simple and predictable but sometimes cutting across semantic boundaries like sentences or tables. Semantic chunking uses structural cues (headings, paragraphs, list items) and sometimes LLM-based segmentation to create chunks that align with meaning, improving attention over coherent units but producing variable sizes that must still respect context limits. Recursive chunking builds a hierarchy: large parent chunks (sections) are subdivided into smaller child chunks, enabling retrieval at multiple granularities and supporting parent-child RAG where the model sees both overview and detail. From a Transformer perspective, semantic and recursive chunking help attention focus on logically related tokens, improving reasoning and reducing the need for aggressive sampling to recover missing context. Fixed-size chunking is easier to implement and faster but may require more sophisticated re-ranking or query transformation to compensate for its lack of semantic awareness.

Question 6

What are embedding models, and how do they convert text to vectors?

Embedding models are neural networks—often Transformer-based—that map tokenized text into dense numerical vectors capturing semantic relationships. They process input tokens through layers of self-attention and feed-forward networks, aggregating contextual information so that similar meanings occupy nearby regions in vector space. Typically, the final hidden states are pooled (e.g., CLS token, mean pooling) to produce a fixed-length embedding for each chunk or query. During training, contrastive or ranking losses encourage the model to place related texts close together and unrelated texts far apart, which makes similarity search effective. In a RAG pipeline, these embeddings are stored in a vector index, and retrieval uses distance metrics to find chunks whose embeddings best match the query, enabling the LLM to reason over semantically relevant context rather than just keyword matches.

Question 7

How do you choose an embedding model for your RAG system?

Choosing an embedding model involves considering language coverage, domain specificity, dimensionality, latency, and compatibility with your LLM and tokenizer. General-purpose Transformer embeddings (e.g., multilingual or instruction-tuned models) work well for broad domains, while domain-specific models trained on technical or legal corpora better capture specialized jargon and relationships. Higher-dimensional embeddings can improve retrieval quality but increase index size and search cost; lower-dimensional models are faster but may lose nuance. You should also align tokenization with the generator model when possible to avoid mismatches in how text is split, and evaluate models using retrieval benchmarks, relevance judgments, and downstream QA performance. Finally, consider deployment constraints: GPU vs CPU inference, batch size, and quantization, all of which affect how quickly embeddings can be computed for new documents and queries.

Question 8

Explain Agentic RAG.

Answer: Agentic RAG. Agentic RAG extends basic retrieval-augmented generation by giving the LLM an “agent” role that can plan, iterate, and call tools (retrievers, databases, APIs) in multiple steps. Instead of a single retrieval followed by one generation, the agent reasons about the query, decomposes it into sub-tasks, and issues retrieval calls as needed, using attention over intermediate results to refine its understanding. The agent may perform query transformation, multi-hop retrieval, and self-reflection, deciding when to fetch more context or when to stop based on internal signals. Training or prompting encourages the model to follow tool-use patterns, and sampling strategies are tuned to balance exploration (trying new retrievals) with exploitation (using existing context). This leads to more robust reasoning over complex knowledge bases, especially when answers require combining multiple documents or interacting with structured data sources.

Question 9

What is hybrid search, and why is it better than pure vector search?

Answer: How does Hybrid Search work? Hybrid search combines dense vector search with traditional lexical or keyword-based retrieval (e.g., BM25), often by scoring and merging results from both modalities. Vector search excels at capturing semantic similarity, even when exact words differ, because embeddings encode meaning via attention over token sequences. However, lexical search can be more precise for rare terms, identifiers, or domain-specific tokens that embeddings may smooth over. Hybrid search leverages both: it may use weighted scoring, reciprocal rank fusion, or re-ranking to integrate signals, improving recall and precision for RAG. This is especially valuable when training data for the embedding model does not fully cover the domain, or when queries mix natural language with structured tokens like error codes or product IDs.

Question 10

What is re-ranking, and how does it improve RAG retrieval quality?

Answer: How does a Reranker work? Re-ranking uses a more powerful model—often a cross-encoder Transformer—to rescore a small set of candidate documents retrieved by a faster first-stage retriever. The reranker jointly attends over the query and each candidate chunk, computing a relevance score that captures fine-grained token interactions and reasoning patterns that simple embedding similarity may miss. By applying this second-stage scoring, the pipeline can correct misordered results, push truly relevant chunks to the top, and filter out noise before context is passed to the generator. This improves the quality of the information the LLM sees, which in turn enhances faithfulness and reduces hallucinations, since attention is focused on better evidence. Re-ranking is a trade-off: it adds latency but often yields significant gains in downstream QA accuracy and user satisfaction.

Question 11

How do you handle multi-document and multi-hop questions in RAG?

Handling multi-document and multi-hop questions requires retrieval and reasoning strategies that can combine information across several chunks. One approach is to retrieve a broader set of candidates, then use the LLM to perform intermediate reasoning steps, such as summarizing each document and then synthesizing them in a second pass. Agentic RAG can decompose the query into sub-questions, retrieving context for each hop and using attention to track entities and relationships across steps. Parent-child chunking and graph-based indices help connect related chunks so the model can follow chains of facts. Sampling strategies may be adjusted to encourage explicit reasoning (e.g., chain-of-thought) while still grounding each step in retrieved evidence, reducing the risk of hallucinated links between documents.

Question 12

What is the "lost in the middle" problem in RAG systems?

The "lost in the middle" problem occurs when relevant information placed in the middle of a long context window receives less attention than tokens at the beginning or end, leading the LLM to overlook crucial facts. Transformer attention patterns and positional encodings can bias the model toward certain regions, especially when prompts include long instructions or multiple documents. In RAG, this manifests when many chunks are concatenated: the model may focus on the first few or the most recent ones, ignoring central evidence. Mitigations include limiting the number of chunks, using re-ranking to prioritize the most relevant ones, structuring prompts with clear headings, and employing techniques like sliding windows or segment-wise reasoning. Some newer architectures and training regimes explicitly address this by learning more uniform attention over long sequences.

Question 13

How do you evaluate a RAG system? Explain faithfulness, relevance, and context precision/recall.

Evaluating RAG involves measuring both retrieval quality and generation behavior. Faithfulness assesses whether the LLM’s answer is supported by the retrieved context and does not introduce unsupported claims; this can be checked via human annotation or automated metrics that compare answer spans to source documents. Relevance measures how well retrieved chunks match the user’s intent, often using IR metrics like nDCG or MRR. Context precision is the fraction of retrieved chunks that are actually useful for answering the question, while context recall measures how many of the truly relevant chunks were retrieved. Together, these metrics reveal whether tokenization, embedding, attention over context, and sampling strategies are working harmoniously. Advanced evaluations may also probe reasoning quality, citation correctness, and robustness to adversarial or ambiguous queries.

Question 14

Explain Self-RAG. How does the model decide when to retrieve?

Self-RAG is a framework where the LLM itself learns to decide whether and when to call the retriever during generation. Instead of always retrieving before answering, the model uses internal signals—such as uncertainty, detection of knowledge gaps, or specific tokens—to trigger retrieval actions. Training may involve reinforcement learning or supervised signals that reward correct decisions about retrieval timing and context usage. The model can interleave reasoning and retrieval: it starts answering, realizes it lacks sufficient information, issues a retrieval call, then incorporates the new context via attention and continues. Sampling strategies and tool-use prompts guide this behavior, enabling more efficient use of the knowledge base and reducing unnecessary retrieval for simple questions while still grounding complex answers.

Question 15

What is GraphRAG, and when would you use it over traditional RAG?

Answer: GraphRAG. GraphRAG organizes knowledge as a graph—nodes representing entities or concepts and edges representing relations— and uses graph-based retrieval and reasoning instead of or alongside flat chunk-based search. Documents are processed to extract entities and relationships, which are stored in a graph database or knowledge graph; queries are mapped to graph traversals or subgraph retrieval. The LLM then attends over structured triples and associated text, enabling more precise multi-hop reasoning and better handling of relational questions. GraphRAG is particularly useful when the domain has rich interconnected data (e.g., biomedical, financial, or enterprise knowledge) where path-based reasoning is critical. It complements traditional RAG by providing a higher-level structure that guides retrieval and reduces the need for the model to infer relationships purely from unstructured text.

Question 16

How do you handle structured data (tables, SQL databases) in a RAG pipeline?

Handling structured data in RAG often involves tool-augmented generation and specialized retrieval. Tables can be serialized into text with schemas and row summaries, then chunked and embedded so the LLM can attend over them, but this may lose some structure. A more robust approach is to expose SQL or analytics engines as tools: the LLM generates queries against the database, retrieves results, and then reasons over them in natural language. For tables, cell-level or column-level embeddings can support fine-grained retrieval, and metadata helps filter by schema or time. Training or prompt engineering teaches the model how to interpret schemas, perform joins, and avoid hallucinating values by always grounding numeric answers in retrieved results. This combination of structured retrieval and Transformer-based reasoning yields accurate, explainable answers over tabular data.

Question 17

What are the common failure modes of RAG systems, and how do you debug them?

Common failure modes include poor retrieval (irrelevant or missing chunks), hallucinated answers despite correct context, outdated information, and misinterpretation of domain-specific language. Debugging starts with inspecting the retrieved documents: check tokenization, chunking boundaries, embedding quality, and metadata filters to see whether the right information is even reaching the LLM. Next, analyze prompts and attention patterns—long, cluttered prompts or “lost in the middle” issues can cause the model to ignore key evidence. Adjust sampling parameters, add explicit instructions to cite sources, and consider fine-tuning or instruction-tuning the model on domain data. Logging retrieval scores, re-ranker outputs, and generated reasoning traces helps pinpoint whether the problem lies in retrieval, generation, or both, guiding targeted fixes rather than ad-hoc tweaks.

Question 18

How do you handle document updates and maintain freshness in a RAG system?

Maintaining freshness requires an ingestion pipeline that can detect changes, re-embed updated content, and update indices without disrupting queries. Documents may carry version metadata and timestamps so retrieval can prefer the latest versions via filters or scoring boosts. Incremental indexing strategies allow only changed chunks to be reprocessed, reducing compute cost; background jobs can batch updates while online queries continue using existing data. The LLM can be prompted to favor recent information and to flag potentially outdated content when multiple versions are present. For time-sensitive domains, combining RAG with live API calls ensures that reasoning is grounded in both static knowledge and real-time data, reducing the risk of stale answers.

Question 19

How do you optimize RAG for latency in production?

Optimizing latency involves tuning every stage: embedding, indexing, retrieval, and generation. Pre-computing and caching document embeddings, using efficient vector indices (e.g., HNSW, IVF), and limiting top-k retrieval reduce search time. On the LLM side, choosing appropriate model sizes, applying quantization, and using batch inference or speculative decoding can significantly speed up generation. Prompt length should be controlled by careful chunking and re-ranking so only the most relevant context is passed to the model, minimizing attention computation. Network and system-level optimizations—such as colocating the vector database and LLM, using GPU acceleration, and parallelizing retrieval and re-ranking—further reduce end-to-end latency while preserving reasoning quality.

Question 20

What is the role of metadata filtering in RAG systems?

Metadata filtering restricts retrieval to subsets of the knowledge base based on attributes like source, date, user, department, or document type. By applying filters before or during vector search, the system ensures that embeddings are compared only within relevant slices, improving precision and reducing noise. This is crucial in multi-tenant or enterprise settings where access control and domain separation matter: the LLM should only attend to chunks the user is allowed to see. Metadata also supports time-aware reasoning, allowing queries to focus on recent versions or specific periods. Combined with attention over filtered context, metadata filtering helps the model reason more accurately and safely, avoiding cross-domain contamination and irrelevant evidence.

Question 21

Compare RAG vs fine-tuning. When would you use each?

Answer: AI Engineering Explained: LLM, RAG, MCP, Agent, Fine-Tuning, Quantization. Fine-tuning modifies the LLM’s weights to internalize new patterns, styles, or domain knowledge, while RAG keeps the model fixed and supplies external context at inference time. Fine-tuning is ideal when you need the model to consistently follow specific instructions, adopt a brand voice, or learn reasoning templates that are hard to express via prompts alone. RAG is better when knowledge changes frequently, is large, or must be kept separate for privacy or compliance reasons; retrieval lets the model attend to relevant facts without memorizing them. In practice, many systems combine both: a lightly fine-tuned Transformer for improved reasoning and instruction following, augmented with RAG for factual grounding. The choice depends on data availability, update frequency, deployment constraints, and whether you want knowledge encoded in weights or in an external store.

Question 22

What is query transformation in RAG (HyDE, query decomposition, step-back prompting)?

Answer: How does HyDE work in RAG? Query transformation modifies the user’s original query to improve retrieval and reasoning. HyDE (Hypothetical Document Embeddings) first asks the LLM to generate a hypothetical answer or document for the query, then embeds that synthetic text and uses it for retrieval, leveraging the model’s semantic understanding to find better matches. Query decomposition breaks complex questions into simpler sub-queries, each of which is retrieved separately; the LLM then combines the results via attention and reasoning. Step-back prompting asks the model to restate the query at a higher level of abstraction, which can align better with how documents are written. These transformations help bridge gaps between user language and corpus language, improving retrieval quality and enabling more robust multi-hop reasoning in RAG pipelines.

Question 23

How do you implement citation and source attribution in RAG?

Implementing citation involves tracking which retrieved chunks contributed to the generated answer and exposing that mapping to the user. During retrieval, each chunk carries identifiers (document IDs, URLs, titles); when the LLM is prompted, these IDs can be embedded in the context or referenced in instructions. The model is then asked to cite specific sources inline or in a separate section, using attention over the context to align statements with evidence. Post-processing can also align answer spans with source text via similarity matching, adding citations even if the model does not explicitly mark them. Proper citation improves trust, enables auditing, and helps detect hallucinations when the model makes claims that cannot be traced back to any retrieved document.

Question 24

How do you scale a RAG system to millions of documents?

Scaling to millions of documents requires efficient indexing, sharding, and retrieval strategies. Vector databases use approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) algorithms like HNSW or IVF to keep search sub-linear in corpus size, and indices may be partitioned by domain or time to reduce search space. Embedding computation is batched and often offloaded to GPUs, with quantization to reduce memory footprint. Metadata filtering and routing ensure queries only hit relevant shards, while caching popular queries and results reduces repeated work. On the LLM side, careful prompt construction and context limits prevent overload when many chunks are candidates. Monitoring retrieval latency, index health, and recall metrics is essential to maintain both performance and reasoning quality at scale.

Question 25

What is parent-child chunking, and how does it improve retrieval?

Parent-child chunking creates a hierarchy where larger parent chunks (e.g., sections or pages) are linked to smaller child chunks (paragraphs, sentences). Retrieval may first identify relevant children via embeddings, then include their parents in the context to provide broader background. This helps the LLM’s attention span both detailed evidence and high-level structure, improving reasoning and reducing the risk of misinterpreting isolated sentences. It also supports multi-hop questions where relationships span multiple children under the same parent. From an indexing perspective, parent-child relationships can be stored as metadata or graph edges, enabling more flexible retrieval strategies that go beyond flat lists of chunks.

Question 26

Your RAG system is hallucinating despite having the right context. How do you fix it?

When hallucinations occur even with correct context, the issue often lies in prompt design, model behavior, or sampling. First, make instructions explicit: tell the LLM to answer only based on provided documents, to say “I don’t know” when evidence is missing, and to cite sources for key claims. Reduce temperature and sampling diversity (e.g., lower top-p) so the model stays closer to deterministic reasoning over the context. Check whether the context is too long or noisy, causing attention to diffuse; re-ranking and tighter top-k can focus the model on the most relevant chunks. In some cases, fine-tuning or instruction-tuning on grounded QA data teaches the model to prioritize evidence over speculation. Logging and analyzing hallucinated outputs helps refine these controls iteratively.

Question 27

Your RAG chunk overlap causes redundant results. How do you reduce redundancy?

Redundant results often arise when overlapping chunks are all retrieved and passed to the LLM. To reduce this, adjust chunking to minimize unnecessary overlap while still preserving context continuity—use sliding windows with smaller strides or semantic boundaries. During retrieval, apply deduplication logic that merges or filters chunks with highly similar embeddings or identical text spans. Re-ranking can penalize near-duplicate candidates so only the most representative chunk is kept. In the prompt, you can also summarize multiple similar chunks into a single consolidated context, reducing token count and focusing attention. These steps streamline reasoning and improve both latency and answer clarity.

Question 28

Your RAG retrieval is too slow with a large knowledge base. How do you speed it up?

To speed up retrieval, use ANN indices optimized for large-scale search, such as HNSW or product quantization, and tune their parameters for your latency/recall trade-off. Shard the index by domain or time and route queries to relevant shards using metadata or learned routing models. Pre-filter candidates with lightweight lexical search or metadata constraints before running expensive vector search. Cache embeddings for frequent queries and precompute results for common patterns. Hardware acceleration (GPUs, fast SSDs) and careful batching of embedding and search operations further reduce latency. All of this ensures that the LLM receives context quickly enough to maintain a responsive user experience while still supporting deep reasoning.

Question 29

Your RAG system returns duplicate results. How do you deduplicate?

Deduplication can be handled at retrieval and post-processing stages. At retrieval time, track document IDs and chunk offsets, and discard candidates that refer to the same text span or overlapping regions beyond a threshold. Use similarity metrics on embeddings or hashed text to detect near-duplicates and keep only the highest-scoring instance. After generation, you can also analyze which sources were cited and collapse repeated references into a single citation list. Proper deduplication reduces context size, improves attention focus, and prevents the model from over-weighting repeated evidence, leading to more balanced reasoning and cleaner answers.

Question 30

Your RAG system needs per-user access control on internal documents. How do you implement it?

Implementing per-user access control requires integrating authentication and authorization with retrieval. Each document and chunk should carry access-control metadata (e.g., user, group, role, tenant), and queries must be executed within a security context that filters candidates accordingly. The vector database or retrieval service enforces these filters before returning results, ensuring the LLM only attends to permitted context. You may also need row-level security for structured data and audit logs to track which documents were accessed during generation. Prompt design should avoid leaking information across users, and multi-tenant deployments must isolate indices or shards. This combination of metadata filtering and secure orchestration keeps reasoning grounded in authorized knowledge only.

Question 31

Your RAG system fails on domain-specific jargon. How do you fix it?

Failures on domain-specific jargon usually stem from embedding models and LLMs that were not trained on the relevant corpus. To fix this, fine-tune or adapt embedding models on domain data so they learn specialized terminology and relationships. You can also fine-tune the LLM with instruction-style examples that explain jargon, map acronyms to definitions, and show correct reasoning patterns. Adding glossaries or terminology tables to the knowledge base and retrieving them alongside other documents helps the model interpret unfamiliar tokens via attention. Query transformation can expand acronyms or rewrite jargon-heavy questions into more general language before retrieval. Together, these steps align tokenization, embeddings, and reasoning with the domain’s vocabulary.

Question 32

Your text-only RAG system now needs to handle images and tables. How do you extend it?

Extending RAG to images and tables involves multimodal models and specialized preprocessing. For images, use vision encoders to generate embeddings or captions that can be stored and retrieved alongside text; multimodal Transformers can then attend over both visual and textual tokens. For tables, preserve structure by encoding headers, cell coordinates, and relationships, or expose table-query tools (like SQL engines) that the LLM can call. Chunking must respect visual and tabular boundaries, and metadata should indicate modality so retrieval can select appropriate content. During generation, prompts should guide the model on how to interpret images and tables, grounding reasoning in both modalities. This turns a text-only RAG pipeline into a richer system capable of answering questions that depend on charts, diagrams, and structured data.

Question 33

Your RAG knowledge base gets updated frequently and needs versioning. How do you manage it?

Managing versioning requires tracking document histories and exposing them through metadata and retrieval logic. Each document and chunk should have version IDs, timestamps, and possibly change logs; older versions can be kept for audit while retrieval defaults to the latest. Metadata filters allow queries to request specific versions (for regulatory or debugging purposes), and scoring can prioritize newer content. Ingestion pipelines must handle deletions, merges, and splits, updating indices incrementally. The LLM can be prompted to mention version information when relevant, helping users understand whether answers reflect the most current state. This combination of version-aware indexing and reasoning keeps RAG outputs consistent with evolving knowledge.

Question 34

Your RAG system fails on multi-hop questions that require combining multiple facts. How do you fix it?

Fixing multi-hop failures involves improving both retrieval connectivity and reasoning. Use query decomposition to break complex questions into simpler sub-queries, retrieving context for each hop separately. GraphRAG or parent-child chunking can encode relationships between entities and documents, making it easier to retrieve connected facts. On the LLM side, encourage chain-of-thought reasoning via prompts or fine-tuning so the model explicitly walks through intermediate steps, attending to different chunks at each stage. Agentic RAG can let the model iteratively retrieve and refine its understanding. Evaluating multi-hop benchmarks and analyzing attention patterns helps identify where reasoning breaks down, guiding targeted improvements.

Question 35

Your enterprise RAG system returns contradictory answers from different source documents. How do you resolve conflicts?

Resolving conflicts requires detecting contradictions and applying policies for reconciliation. The LLM can be prompted to compare sources explicitly, highlighting differences and reasoning about which is more authoritative based on metadata like source type, date, and trust level. Retrieval can prioritize high-quality or canonical documents via scoring boosts, reducing the chance that outdated or low-trust sources dominate attention. In some cases, the correct behavior is to present multiple viewpoints and explain the discrepancy rather than choosing one answer. Logging conflicts and feeding them back into curation processes helps improve the knowledge base over time. This combination of metadata-aware retrieval and reflective reasoning keeps enterprise RAG outputs aligned with organizational truth.

Question 36

Your RAG system returns outdated answers from an evolving knowledge base. How do you keep it current?

Keeping answers current requires time-aware retrieval and aggressive index maintenance. Ensure that ingestion pipelines promptly process new documents and mark old ones as superseded, updating embeddings and indices incrementally. Use metadata filters and scoring that favor recent content, and prompt the LLM to consider timestamps when reasoning. For highly dynamic domains, combine RAG with live API calls or streaming data sources so the model can ground answers in real-time information. Monitoring queries that frequently hit outdated documents helps identify gaps in the update process. Together, these measures align the Transformer’s reasoning with the freshest available context.

Question 37

Your RAG system struggles with PDF documents containing tables and layouts. How do you fix PDF parsing?

Fixing PDF parsing involves using more advanced extraction tools that preserve structure, not just raw text. Layout-aware parsers detect columns, headers, footers, and table boundaries, reconstructing logical reading order and tabular relationships. Once extracted, tables can be serialized with explicit row/column markers or stored in structured formats for downstream retrieval. Chunking should respect these structures, avoiding splits that mix unrelated cells or columns. Embedding models can then capture both textual content and layout cues, and the LLM can be prompted to interpret tables correctly. For complex PDFs, manual curation or semi-automated validation may be necessary to ensure that the knowledge base reflects the document’s true semantics before RAG reasoning is applied.
